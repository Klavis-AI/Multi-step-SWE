# Specification material — step 13

The full text of every RFC this task touches is in this directory,
byte-identical to the IETF originals (`SHA256SUMS` covers them). It is
here so the milestone can be worked offline and so that every step reads
the same words. This environment has no general network access, so these
documents and what the image already holds are all the reference material
there is.

What is load-bearing *for this step*:

| document | sections | why it matters here |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `rfc9000.txt` | 2.2 (sending and receiving data), 2.4 (operations on streams), 4 (flow control), 4.1 (data flow control), 4.3 (flow control performance) | 2.2 is why a send call may accept part of what it is offered and no more. Stream data is an ordered byte stream that the sender hands over in whatever sized pieces it likes; nothing at the protocol level preserves the boundaries between calls, and nothing obliges the endpoint to take a whole piece at once. Read it before reasoning about `stream_send_zc`'s tail: the split point is an implementation's capacity decision, not a protocol event, and the receiver cannot tell where it happened. 2.4 is the sending-side API this crate is a realisation of -- write data, close, reset -- and is worth re-reading here because a zero-copy send is the same operation with a different ownership story, not a new one. In particular `fin` is a property of the byte stream rather than of any one write, which is why it lands at whatever offset the write reached and why the bytes left over afterwards are past the final size rather than merely unsent. 4 and 4.1 are the two limits that decide how much a send can accept: the per-stream limit and the connection-wide limit, both advertised by the receiver, both only ever increased. They are the reason a send that returns a tail is a normal outcome rather than an error, and the reason `Error::Done` means "nothing right now" rather than "never". 4.3 is the counterpart to `set_send_capacity_factor` seen from the protocol. It is about the receiver's limits being large enough to keep the sender busy; the factor is about the *sender's* willingness to take bytes from its application before the congestion controller has room for them. Reading 4.3 makes clear that the factor cannot make the connection faster than the receiver allows -- it changes where the data waits, not how much may fly. |
| `rfc9002.txt` | 7 (congestion control), 7.2 (initial and minimum congestion window), 7.3 (congestion control states), 7.7 (pacing), 7.8 (underutilizing the congestion window) | Section 7 is the framing this step's fifth algorithm sits inside. It defines the congestion window as a limit on bytes in flight and states plainly that QUIC's recommended controller is one option among many: endpoints MAY use another, provided it follows the ECN and persistent-congestion requirements. That is the licence under which a crate ships five, and it is why adding a fifth is not a protocol change at all -- nothing on the wire says which one is running, which is exactly why the API has to expose `gcongestion_enabled` for a caller to find out. 7.2 and 7.3 are the vocabulary the algorithm names are shorthand for: initial window, minimum window, slow start, recovery, congestion avoidance. Read them for what all five implementations have in common, so that "BBRv2 versus the gcongestion BBRv2" is understood as two implementations of one job rather than two behaviours. 7.7 is pacing, which is the part of the job the two BBR implementations differ most in, and 7.8 is the section that `set_send_capacity_factor` exists to act on. 7.8 describes a sender that is limited by its own application or by its own flow control rather than by congestion, and observes that such a sender's congestion window says nothing useful about the path. A capacity factor above 1.0 is one answer to that: let the application hand over more than the window currently justifies, so the window is not underutilised the moment it opens. Read 7.8 for why this is a buffering decision and not a congestion-control one. |
| `rfc9114.txt` | 4.1 (HTTP message framing), 7.2.1 (DATA frames), 7.2.2 (HEADERS frames) | 4.1 is the permitted shape of an HTTP/3 message as a sequence of frames: an optional run of informational header sections, the final header section, then DATA, then an optional trailer section. It is the whole of the ordering contract that `send_body_zc` and `send_additional_headers_with_priority` are checked against -- a body before any header section, or a second trailer section, are violations of this sequence and not of any rule those two methods invent. 4.1 is also where the client/server asymmetry comes from, and it is worth reading rather than taking on trust: informational responses are a response construct, so a request may not carry one, which is why a client calling the additional-headers method with `is_trailer_section == false` is refused before anything else is looked at. 7.2.1 is the DATA frame, and it is the reason a zero-copy body send has to think about prefixes at all: the payload is preceded by a type and a length, so the bytes the application owns are not the bytes that go on the wire unless something can put a header in front of them. `BufSplit::try_add_prefix` is that something, and a buffer that refuses is not an error -- it just means the header is written as its own piece, and 7.2.1 is what makes those two arrangements indistinguishable to the receiver. 7.2.2 is the HEADERS frame. Nothing on the wire distinguishes an informational, final or trailing section, so a receiver tells them apart by position -- which is why the case reads them in order and compares them in order. |
| `rfc9218.txt` | 4 (priority parameters), 4.1 (urgency), 4.2 (incremental), 6 (reprioritization), 10 (server scheduling) | 4.1 is the arithmetic behind the clamp. Urgency is an integer **between 0 and 7 inclusive**, in descending order of priority, and the default is 3 -- which is also why `Priority::default()` equals `Priority::new(3, false)` and not something else. A three-bit field has no representation for 200, so the choice is between rejecting the value and clamping it; this crate clamps, and knowing the field's range is what tells you the clamped result is 7 rather than 200 mod 8. 4.2 is the other field: incremental is a boolean, it is independent of urgency, and it changes how a server may interleave responses at the *same* urgency. That independence is why two `Priority` values with equal urgency and different incremental flags are not equal. 6 is reprioritization -- the statement that a priority may be signalled again later in the life of a request, and that the signals are cumulative and last-one-wins. It is the reason there is a *send headers and set priority* method at all rather than only a priority-at-request-time argument. 10 is the disclaimer that makes all of this testable. Priority parameters are a suggestion; a server is not required to act on them and a client cannot observe scheduling from the outside. Read it before expecting a test to assert ordering: what is checkable is that the call is accepted, that the header sections arrive intact and in order, and that an out-of-range urgency does not break either. |
